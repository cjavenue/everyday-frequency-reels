# FACTS — The Sri Lankan leopard

Every number and claim in the film, with its source and date. Checked 28 Nov 2026.

| # | Claim in the film | Source (date) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sri Lanka has no tigers or lions; the leopard is its only big cat | Fernando et al., "Understanding the Rising Threat to Sri Lankan Leopards in Central Hills", Univ. of Sri Jayewardenepura FE Symposium (2024): "the only big cat species found in Sri Lanka" | "Big cat" = genus *Panthera*. Sri Lanka's small wild cats (fishing, rusty-spotted, jungle cat) are not big cats. |
| 2 | Found nowhere else on Earth (endemic subspecies *Panthera pardus kotiya*, described 1956) | Deraniyagala, *Spolia Zeylanica* 28 (1956); IUCN Red List, Kittle & Watson (2020), e.T15959A50660847 | The subspecies is endemic; the species *Panthera pardus* lives elsewhere. |
| 3 | One of only two island-living leopards on Earth | Kittle et al., *Global Ecology and Conservation* 37, e02173 (Sep 2022) | The other is the Javan leopard. |
| 4 | The island's top (apex) predator, with no bigger rival; rarely stores kills in trees | Kittle et al., *Global Ecology and Conservation* (2022): "the only apex predator in Sri Lanka"; Kittle, Watson & Fernando, *Tropical Ecology* 58 (2017) | Kill-hoisting is shown visually only; not narrated as a cause. |
| 5 | Yala Block 1: at least 77 leopards, about 0.55 per km² (≈ one per two km²) | "A Multi-Point Identification Approach for the Recognition of Individual Leopards", *Animals* (MDPI), 2022, PMC8909430 — minimum population on 31 Mar 2021 | "One of the highest densities known": Mongabay, 23 May 2025 (Kumana study report). |
| 6 | Tawny coat with close-set rosettes | IUCN Red List (2020); Wikipedia "Sri Lankan leopard" | |
| 7 | Males average 56 kg (heaviest measured 77 kg) | Pocock, *Fauna of British India* (1939): 11 males measured | Historic measurements; film says "about fifty-six kilos". |
| 8 | In the dry zone it mostly eats spotted (axis) deer | Kittle, Watson & Fernando, *Tropical Ecology* 58 (2017) | |
| 9 | Lives across the whole island: dry scrub, rainforest, tea estates in the hills | Kittle et al., *Biodiversity & Conservation* 27 (2018); Kittle et al., *Cat News* 56 (2014) | |
| 10 | Fewer than 1,000 adults remain; IUCN status Vulnerable | IUCN Red List, Kittle & Watson (2020): Vulnerable, < 800 mature individuals; WWCT estimate "about 1,000" (Mongabay, May 2025); "fewer than 1,000 mature leopards" (Mongabay, 11 Jun 2026) | Estimate. Film says "fewer than a thousand adults". |
| 11 | 164 human-caused leopard deaths recorded, 2008–2024 | Weerakkody et al., *Wildlife Letters* (2026), doi:10.1002/wll2.70040; reported by Mongabay, 11 Jun 2026, and Sunday Times (LK), 24 May 2026 | Recorded deaths only; true toll likely higher. |
| 12 | Wire snares caused more than six in ten deaths with a known cause (62.3%) | Same study (2026) | Snares are usually set for deer, wild boar or crop pests. |
| 13 | Nearly 40% of deaths were in one district, Nuwara Eliya, which is 4.4% of the leopard's range | Same study (2026), via Mongabay, 11 Jun 2026 | On screen: "≈40% of deaths · 4.4% of range". |
| 14 | Most deaths happen outside protected areas | Mongabay, Aug 2025 (DWC records 2001–2023: nearly three in four deaths outside protected areas) | |
| 15 | Actions: forest links between habitats; snare removal; protecting livestock at night; keeping distance on safari | Kittle (Mongabay, Jun 2026): conservation must extend beyond parks, coexistence; DWC snare-removal raids (Mongabay, Jun 2026); retaliation for livestock losses is a known cause (IUCN 2020); Kittle et al. (2018): distribution tied to forest cover and connectivity | Advice framed as general conservation actions, not a single official programme. |

Dropped (could not verify or not needed): leopards "up to 100 kg" (BBC Earth 2014 — evidence lacking); black-leopard counts; 2025 death totals (differing news figures).
