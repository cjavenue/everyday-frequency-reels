# FACTS — Whales of Mirissa

Every number and claim used in the film, with its source and date. Checked 28 Nov 2026.

| # | Claim in the film | Source (date) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The blue whale is the largest animal that has ever lived. | AP via SBS News, "Pact to save Sri Lankan whales from ships" (Mar 2019): "the biggest animal ever known to have lived on the planet"; IFAW press release (8 Sep 2022): "the largest animal on the planet". | Widely accepted scientific statement. |
| 2 | Off Mirissa the continental shelf is narrow; its edge lies about 7–8 km south of Mirissa. | de Vos et al., *J. Cetacean Res. Manage.* (IWC journal, article 236): "The edge of the continental shelf lies approximately 7–8km South of Mirissa". | Screen text: "Shelf edge ≈ 7–8 km out". |
| 3 | Deep water and upwelling along the shelf edge feed plankton, and in turn tiny shrimp-like animals (zooplankton/krill) the whales eat. | Ilangakoon, Kyoto Univ. repository, SEASTAR2000 proc. (2013): whales off southern Sri Lanka "due to a narrow continental shelf and upwelling, related feeding opportunities"; Mongabay (30 Apr 2025): upwelling from sea canyons supports phytoplankton, then zooplankton and shrimp. | Worded generally ("tiny shrimp-like animals"). |
| 4 | Blue whales and sperm whales off Mirissa prefer the outer shelf and the steep slope; blue whales feed there. | UEA research portal, "Cetacean occurrence and diversity in whale watching waters off Mirissa" (Priyadarshana et al.). | |
| 5 | These are northern Indian Ocean blue whales; unlike other blue whales they don't migrate to cooler/polar waters, and at least some stay around Sri Lanka all year. | de Vos et al., *J. Marine Biology* (2016), NOAA repository 63191: "Year-round sightings… suggest that at least a portion of this population remains resident within Sri Lankan waters"; de Vos (2015, NOAA SWFSC): "the NIO population does not migrate annually to cooler waters". | Said as "some stay all year", not "all". |
| 6 | Mirissa's whale-watching season runs November to April, when the sea is calm. | Lakpura (whale watching page, 2026) and multiple 2026 travel guides; operator sighting logs (Mirissa Water Sports, 2025/26 season). | Sightings are never guaranteed (not claimed). |
| 7 | One of the world's busiest shipping lanes passes close to Sri Lanka's south coast, through the area with the most whales. | IFAW press release (8 Sep 2022): lanes off Dondra Head "take ships right through the area with the most whales"; Britannica/IFAW (Leaper): "one of the world's busiest shipping lanes". | |
| 8 | Ship strikes are the main threat to this population. | de Vos et al. 2016 (*J. Marine Biology*): "we ranked ship strike as the most important population-level threat"; IFAW: "the key threat". | |
| 9 | Moving the lane 15 nautical miles south could cut the risk of collisions by about 95%. | IFAW, surveys 2014–2015 with University of Ruhuna, WTI, Biosphere Foundation, Raja and the Whales; paper in *Regional Studies in Marine Science*: >1,000 interactions a year could fall to ~50 (95%). | "About 95%" — a modelled estimate. Labelled "estimate" on screen. |
| 10 | In 2022 MSC, the world's largest container line, rerouted its ships about 15 nautical miles south. | IFAW press release (8 Sep 2022); Mongabay (Oct 2022). | Voice says "In twenty twenty-two". |

## Dropped (couldn't be verified well enough, or contested)
- Depth figures ("1 km deep just offshore") — only from tour sites.
- Population size (600–1,500, AP 2019) — an old estimate with a wide range.
- Recent decline in sightings (Mongabay 2025 reports fewer sightings since 2018, but 2025/26 operator logs show regular sightings) — contested, left out.
- Exact ships per day (100 vs 200 vs 50,000/year in different sources) — inconsistent.
