## Facebook description
Most of the cinnamon sold around the world isn't true cinnamon. It's cassia, a cheaper relative. True cinnamon, Cinnamomum verum, is native to Sri Lanka, and the island still grows about 80–90% of the world's supply. Empires fought over it: first the Portuguese, then the Dutch East India Company, which ran it as a monopoly from 1658 to 1796. In 2025 Sri Lanka's cinnamon exports reached a record of about $260 million.

What makes it different? A Ceylon stick is many paper-thin layers of inner bark rolled together like a cigar, while cassia is one thick, hard curl. It tastes sweeter and gentler, with a hint of citrus. The bigger difference is hidden: coumarin. Cassia can hold around 100 times more, and Ceylon has only traces. Too much coumarin can harm the liver, and just 2 g of cassia a day reaches the EU's tolerable daily intake for a 60 kg adult.

How is it made? Coppiced bushes send up new shoots that are cut twice a year, after the monsoons. A peeler scrapes the outer bark, rubs the stem with a brass rod and lifts the inner bark in one piece. The strips are nested together and dried in the shade into quills. The finest grade, Alba, is under 6 mm across, and since 2022 "Ceylon Cinnamon" has been a protected name in the EU.

Sources: Sri Lanka Department of Cinnamon Development via Daily Mirror and Daily FT (2026); PLOS One (Dec 2025); EU Regulation 2022/144; BfR (German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment); AGES (Austria); J Agric Food Chem (2013); Sri Lanka Export Development Board; JSTOR Daily.
