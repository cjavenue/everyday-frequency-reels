# FACTS — Sigiriya: the untold stories

Checked 27 Nov 2026. Each claim used in the film, with its source. Where sources differ, the film uses the cautious wording shown.

| # | Claim in the film | Source(s) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sigiriya is a granite rock about 180 m high ("the Lion's Rock") | UNESCO World Heritage listing 202, quoted by Facts and Details; Britannica | Some sources say "nearly 200 m"; the film uses UNESCO's 180 m |
| 2 | King Kashyapa I (Kassapa) reigned 477–495 CE and built his capital on and around the rock | UNESCO (via Facts and Details); Britannica; Wikivoyage | Wikipedia's Kashyapa page gives 473–495; the film uses UNESCO's dates |
| 3 | Kashyapa was the son of King Dhatusena by a non-royal wife; his half-brother Moggallana was the heir | Wikipedia (Kashyapa I, citing the Cūḷavaṃsa); Central Cultural Fund site page | |
| 4 | The chronicle says Kashyapa had his father killed by walling him up alive | Cūḷavaṃsa, as summarised by Wikipedia (Kashyapa I) and Sunday Observer (2002) | Told as "the chronicle says"; other accounts differ (e.g. buried in the bund of Kala Wewa) |
| 5 | Moggallana fled to South India to raise an army; Kashyapa moved his capital to the rock | Wikipedia (Sigiriya, Kashyapa I) | |
| 6 | The final ascent once climbed between the paws and into the mouth of a giant brick lion; only the paws and first steps remain | Lonely Planet (Lion's Paws); Wikipedia (Sigiriya) | |
| 7 | The paws were dug out by archaeologist H.C.P. Bell in 1898 | Lonely Planet (Lion's Paws); Lonely Planet FR; ThePrint (excavation 1895–1898) | |
| 8 | Sigiriya / Sinhagiri means "Lion Rock" | Lonely Planet; National Geographic | |
| 9 | The graffiti mention about 500 painted women; about 20 survive today | Wikipedia (Sigiriya: "references in the graffiti to 500 ladies"); Rough Guides (21 survive); National Geographic (21) | Counts vary 18–22 by source, so the film says "about twenty" |
| 10 | Many scholars read the women as apsaras (celestial nymphs); others as court ladies | Rough Guides; National Geographic | Shown as an open question |
| 11 | The frescoes were vandalised with paint in October 1967 and restored | Sunday Times (2008); Wikipedia; Raja de Silva via Island/eLanka | Not used in narration (time) |
| 12 | Visitors scratched verses on the Mirror Wall, mostly 8th–10th centuries CE, most addressed to the painted women | Paranavitana, *Sigiri Graffiti* (OUP, 1956); Sunday Times (2008); Univ. of Kelaniya journal (2021) | Some verses are as early as the 6th century and as late as the 13th; the film says "over a thousand years ago" |
| 13 | Senarat Paranavitana read and published 685 verses in 1956 | Sunday Observer (2009); wisdomlib (Sigiriya gallery); Cambridge (JRAS review) | |
| 14 | Benille Priyanka later read about 800 more | Sunday Times (2010: "800 hitherto unread records"); Sunday Times (2008: "another 820") | The film says "about eight hundred more" |
| 15 | The water gardens are among the oldest landscaped gardens in the world | BBC via eLanka (2021); Central Province tourism (gov.lk); New World Encyclopedia | |
| 16 | The fountains are fed by underground conduits using gravity and pressure, and still rise in the rainy season | wisdomlib (Water Garden layout plan); Central Cultural Fund (ccf.gov.lk); BBC via eLanka | |
| 17 | 2025 cleaning confirmed the moat, which sits slightly higher, feeds the fountains | The Morning (3 Oct 2025), quoting CCF Director General Nilan Cooray | |
| 18 | Kashyapa's capital lasted about 18 years | India Environment Portal / Down To Earth (1995); 477–495 dates | |
| 19 | In the final battle (495 CE) Kashyapa turned his war elephant away from a swamp; his army took it as retreat and fled; he took his own life with his dagger | Cūḷavaṃsa, quoted in Sunday Observer (16 Jun 2002); Wikipedia (Kashyapa I) | Chronicle account; told as "the chronicle says" |
| 20 | Afterwards the site became a Buddhist monastery until about the 13th–14th century | Wikipedia (Sigiriya); New World Encyclopedia; Peradeniya repository | |
| 21 | Monks lived in the rock caves long before Kashyapa (from about the 3rd century BCE) | Wikivoyage; Facts and Details | |
| 22 | Former Commissioner of Archaeology Dr Raja de Silva argued Sigiriya was a Buddhist monastery, not a palace (book, 2002) | Powell's (publisher synopsis); Sunday Observer (2002); Colombo Telegraph (Nov 2026); Daily FT (Aug 2026) | A minority view; the mainstream view is still Kashyapa's palace-fortress. ## Dropped (could not verify, or disputed)
- "Built by Ravana" and other legend claims — not historical.
- "Nearly 100 pools" — single travel source.
- Kashyapa "poisoned by a concubine" — minor variant, no primary source.
- Exact fresco area (5,600 m²) — sources disagree widely (42 m to 140 m long).
